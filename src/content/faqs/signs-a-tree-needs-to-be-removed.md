Most trees that fail were showing warning signs long before they came down. The trouble is that the signs are easy to live beside without noticing, because they develop slowly. Here is what we look at when we assess a tree in Bowling Green, roughly in order of how seriously we take it.

## Signs That Need Attention Now

A lean that has changed. A tree that has grown at an angle its whole life is usually fine. A tree that is leaning more than it did last year is a different matter entirely, and it is one of the clearest danger signals there is.

Soil heaving or cracking at the base. If the ground on one side of the trunk is lifting, mounding, or has opened up a crescent-shaped crack, the root plate is moving. That tree is in the process of failing, and it should be looked at immediately rather than next month.

A vertical crack or seam in the trunk. A split running up and down the stem means the trunk is coming apart. Combined with a lean or a heavy one-sided canopy, it is a serious hazard.

Mushrooms or shelf fungus at the base. Fungal conks growing out of the trunk or root flare are the visible fruit of decay that is already well advanced inside the tree. By the time you see them on the outside, a meaningful share of the structural wood may already be gone.

## Signs Worth Getting Assessed Soon

Large dead limbs in the upper canopy. One or two dead branches on an otherwise healthy tree usually just need pruning. Widespread deadwood through the top, sometimes called stag-heading, suggests the tree is declining as a whole.

A canopy that thinned out of season. If a tree leafed out sparsely this spring, or dropped its leaves noticeably earlier than its neighbors, something is wrong at the roots or in the vascular system.

Hollows and cavities. A hollow trunk is not automatically fatal — plenty of hollow trees stand for decades, because strength lives in the outer wood. What matters is how much sound wood remains around the cavity and whether the tree has anything to hit.

Weak V-shaped unions. Where two stems meet in a tight V rather than a broad U, bark often gets trapped in the join and the union never bonds properly. These split under wind or ice load, frequently taking half the tree with them.

Suckers around the base. A sudden flush of shoots from the root collar is often a stress response — the tree attempting to regrow after something has gone badly wrong above.

## Local Problems We See Around Warren County

Ash trees. The emerald ash borer has moved through Kentucky and killed ash across the region. If you have an ash showing thinning canopy, D-shaped exit holes in the bark, or heavy woodpecker activity, it is almost certainly infested. Dead ash becomes brittle unusually fast, which makes late removal both harder and more expensive.

Bradford pears. Planted heavily through local subdivisions and notorious for splitting at the crotch once mature. If you have one over twenty years old near a driveway or roof, it is worth a look before storm season rather than after.

Shallow rooting over limestone. Warren County’s karst geology means bedrock is often close to the surface and roots spread wide rather than deep. Saturated spring soil plus high wind is what puts otherwise healthy-looking trees on the ground here.

## Where the Tree Stands Matters as Much as Its Condition

Risk is a combination of how likely a tree is to fail and what it would hit. A hollow tree at the back of five acres can often be left alone and allowed to become wildlife habitat. The same tree twenty feet from a bedroom is a different decision entirely. When we assess a tree we are weighing both.

## When a Tree Can Be Kept

Plenty of trees that worry homeowners do not need removing. Deadwood can be pruned out. Storm-damaged limbs can be cleaned up properly so the wounds seal. Some leans are entirely stable. We would far rather tell you your tree is fine than sell you a removal you did not need — and if it can be saved with pruning, we will say so.
