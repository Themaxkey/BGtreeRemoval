For most homeowners the short answer is no. If the tree is growing on your own private property inside Bowling Green, you generally do not need a permit from the city to have it removed. Where people get caught out is the trees that look like they are on their property but legally are not, and a handful of private restrictions that have nothing to do with the city at all.

## Trees on Your Own Property

Bowling Green’s tree ordinance is written around public trees rather than private ones. The city’s rules on removal apply to trees in the public right-of-way and on city property, so a maple in your back yard or a dying oak beside your driveway is normally yours to deal with as you see fit.

That said, ordinances do get amended, and unincorporated parts of Warren County can be treated differently from addresses inside the city limits. If your tree is large, prominent, or you have any doubt at all, one phone call to the City of Bowling Green before work starts costs you nothing and settles it.

## Right-of-Way Trees Are a Different Story

This is the one that surprises people. The strip of grass between the sidewalk and the street — the one you mow, water, and rake — is frequently public right-of-way rather than your land, even though it sits in front of your house and you have maintained it for years.

Trees in that strip are public trees. Removing, cutting, or disturbing one requires approval from the City of Bowling Green first. And because the adjoining property owner is treated as responsible for what happens there, an unauthorized removal or a botched job on a right-of-way tree can come back on you, not just on whoever swung the saw.

If your tree sits anywhere near that boundary, find out which side of the line it is on before booking the work. We are happy to take a look and give you our read, but the city is the authority.

## Private Restrictions the City Has Nothing to Do With

Even where no permit is needed, other rules can still apply:

HOA and subdivision covenants. Plenty of Bowling Green subdivisions have deed restrictions covering tree removal and replacement. These are private agreements, entirely separate from city code, and your HOA will not care that the city had no objection.

Rental property. If you rent, the decision belongs to the property owner. Get it in writing.

Commercial sites and new developments. Approved site and landscape plans often specify trees that must be preserved or replaced. Removing a protected tree can put a certificate of occupancy at risk, which is a far bigger problem than a permit.

Utility easements. An easement across your property may limit what can be done within it, and any tree contacting a power line is a matter for your electric provider before it is a matter for a tree service.

## Boundary Trees and Your Neighbor’s Tree

A tree whose trunk straddles the property line is generally considered to belong to both owners, and neither can unilaterally remove it. If the trunk is entirely on your neighbor’s side, it is their tree even where the branches hang over your roof. You typically may trim back growth that crosses onto your property, but you cannot cut in a way that kills the tree, and destroying a neighbor’s tree can carry real financial liability.

Our strong advice is to talk to the neighbor before anyone starts cutting. These disputes get expensive and they last for years.

## Emergencies

When a tree has come down on a house or is actively threatening people, making the site safe comes first. Do not leave a hazardous tree standing while you wait on paperwork. Call us, and if a line is involved call your electric provider as well.

## Not Sure? Ask Before You Cut

Rules change and every property is a little different, so please treat this page as general guidance rather than a legal ruling — confirm anything uncertain with the City of Bowling Green or the City-County Planning Commission.
